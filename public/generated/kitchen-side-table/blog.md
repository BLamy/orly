# The Side Table

*Weekend Builds №4 — grounded in Charles Mak's [How to Build a Custom Kitchen Side Table](https://www.leevalley.com/en-ca/discover/woodworking/how-to-build-a-custom-kitchen-side-table) for Lee Valley: a two-compartment side table in 1×8 and 2×4 lumber, told through six shop techniques rather than a cut list.*

## Chapter 1 · Mark Before You Mill

The piece is a kitchen side table in 1×8 and 2×4 lumber with two compartments — the upper one holding a pair of storage baskets, the lower one for bottles. Counting the top and the lid, it comes to around sixteen parts.

Sixteen parts of milled softwood have a quiet problem: once they are cut, they all look the same. Which face was out? Which edge was jointed to which?

<figure><img src="/generated/kitchen-side-table/blog/chapter-1-2.png" alt="Four boards arranged as a panel beside an elevation of the two-compartment table"><figcaption>Four boards laid out in the order they are meant to be glued — and nothing about them says so.</figcaption></figure>

The first technique in the article answers it before it happens. Mark the components: draw a single triangle straight across the whole group at once, in exactly the arrangement you want. Not one mark per board — one mark spanning all of them.

<figure><img src="/generated/kitchen-side-table/blog/chapter-1-3.png" alt="A single triangle drawn across all four boards at once"><figcaption>One mark, four boards, one arrangement.</figcaption></figure>

Then the boards come apart to be jointed and planed and stood against a wall, and each carries its own fragment of that triangle. When one goes back turned around, every edge still fits and the grain still looks plausible — but the lines do not meet.

<figure><img src="/generated/kitchen-side-table/blog/chapter-1-5.png" alt="Boards reassembled with one turned around, the triangle visibly broken"><figcaption>A mark that means nothing on any single board, and is obviously wrong the moment the group is.</figcaption></figure>

That is the whole trick, and it is why every group of parts in the table gets one: the top, the compartment bottoms, the side frames. Marking out the components prevents most milling and assembly errors before they can happen.

## Chapter 2 · Wide From Narrow

The top and both compartment bottoms are wider than any 1×8 you can buy, so they are not bought wide — they are made wide, by gluing 1×8 boards edge to edge.

Each joint is glue on two long-grain edges, which is more than strong enough; a well-fitted edge joint outlasts the wood beside it. What it is not is self-aligning. Clamps pull the boards together from the sides, and that side pressure has to go somewhere: push hard on a row of thin boards and the panel lifts in the middle.

<figure><img src="/generated/kitchen-side-table/blog/chapter-2-3.png" alt="An end-on panel buckling upward under side clamp pressure"><figcaption>Side pressure alone will happily glue you a curved panel.</figcaption></figure>

Cauls are the fix — stiff bars clamped across the panel above and below, at right angles to the joints, holding it flat while it cures without squeezing the joints themselves. Scrap offcuts go between the clamp jaws and the wood, because this is softwood and a steel jaw leaves a dent you would be steaming out later.

<figure><img src="/generated/kitchen-side-table/blog/chapter-2-4.png" alt="Cauls clamped above and below the panel, holding it flat"><figcaption>Cauls take the buckling out; scrap pads keep the jaws off the wood.</figcaption></figure>

Squeezing the joints pushes glue out of every one of them, which is a good sign — it means the joint is full — and also the thing that ruins a finish. It comes off with a chisel and a scraper: not a wet rag, which spreads it into the grain, and not a sander after it has cured into something closer to glass than wood.

<figure><img src="/generated/kitchen-side-table/blog/chapter-2-6.png" alt="Beads of squeeze-out along every joint"><figcaption>Full joints, and a bead at each one to deal with before it hardens.</figcaption></figure>

## Chapter 3 · One Setting, Every Cut

All the joinery is loose-tenon joinery cut with a Domino: legs to aprons, butt joints, and connections out in the middle of a board where nothing else would hold. The machine knows one setting at a time — a fence height, a width, a depth. Changing it is quick; changing it and then getting it back *exactly* is not.

Work part by part, in the order the parts occur to you, and twelve cuts can mean eleven separate resets — every one of them a chance to be slightly off.

<figure><img src="/generated/kitchen-side-table/blog/chapter-3-3.png" alt="A queue of twelve cuts in arbitrary order with a reset marked between nearly every one"><figcaption>Twelve cuts taken as they come: eleven machine resets.</figcaption></figure>

Mak's advice is one line: finish all the cuts under one machine setting before changing it. Which is really a sort — group the identical cuts, then run the groups. Same twelve cuts, same afternoon, two resets instead of eleven, and every joint of a given kind cut without the fence moving between them.

<figure><img src="/generated/kitchen-side-table/blog/chapter-3-5.png" alt="The same twelve cuts grouped by setting, needing only two resets"><figcaption>The identical work, reordered.</figcaption></figure>

The same instinct solves the hardest joint in the piece. The lower compartment's divider has to land on a wide bottom panel, and mortises cut into two loose parts almost never line up — so the divider is clamped to the compartment bottom first, its mortises cut with it held exactly where it will live, and the matching mortises in the panel cut from that same reference.

<figure><img src="/generated/kitchen-side-table/blog/chapter-3-7.png" alt="The divider clamped to the compartment bottom while matching mortises are cut in both"><figcaption>Clamp first, then mortise: both halves referenced off one position.</figcaption></figure>

And while the machine is out, one more setting: 10 mm slots on the inside of the upper aprons and the back. Not joints at all — that is how the top gets attached later.

## Chapter 4 · Square, Round and Flat

Nothing here is glued up all at once. The side frames go first — two legs with an upper and a lower apron — then the compartments, then everything together. The reason is that a four-piece frame will cheerfully glue up as a parallelogram: every joint tight, every part the right length, and the whole thing leaning.

So you measure the diagonals, corner to corner both ways, and nudge it in the clamps until the two numbers match.

<figure><img src="/generated/kitchen-side-table/blog/chapter-4-2.png" alt="A side frame with both diagonals measured and compared"><figcaption>Equal diagonals is the only cheap proof of square you get.</figcaption></figure>

Then the top's edges are rounded over, and cut order matters again. A router bit blows out a chip where it exits a cut, and end grain splits worst. Rout the long sides first and the last pass leaves a torn corner with nothing left to remove it. Rout the ends first — tear-out and all — and the side passes cut straight through the damage and take it away.

<figure><img src="/generated/kitchen-side-table/blog/chapter-4-6.png" alt="The ends routed first, then the sides removing the blown-out corners"><figcaption>Same two cuts, opposite order, no repair.</figcaption></figure>

Dents get the same treatment: a dent is not missing wood, it is crushed wood, so a damp rag and a hot iron steam the fibres back up — before the final sanding to 220 grit, not after, since sanding first only levels the surface down to the bottom of the dent.

<figure><img src="/generated/kitchen-side-table/blog/chapter-4-8.png" alt="A damp rag and hot iron raising a dent in the top"><figcaption>Steam it up first; sand it flat second.</figcaption></figure>

Finally the top is fastened with cabinetmaker's buttons — small rabbeted blocks milled from strips on the table saw, tongues dropped into those 10 mm slots and screwed up into the top. A wide panel changes width with the seasons; screwed down hard it will crack itself trying, and a button holds it firmly down while letting it slide.

<figure><img src="/generated/kitchen-side-table/blog/chapter-4-11.png" alt="A button hooked into an apron slot, allowing the top to move"><figcaption>Held down, not held still.</figcaption></figure>

A maker's mark goes on before it heads off for staining — which is a small thing, and also the moment a pile of 1×8s becomes somebody's furniture.
