# The Pallet Bar

*Weekend Builds №2 — grounded in Lee Valley's [Make a Backyard Bar Using Pallets](https://www.leevalley.com/en-ca/discover/woodworking/make-a-backyard-bar-using-pallets), a first-time builder's account of turning three reclaimed pallets, one old shelf and a single purchased board into an outdoor bar.*

## Chapter 1 · Choose the Pallet, Skip the Cut

Most builds start at a lumberyard. This one starts at a salvage pile, and that changes what the first decision is. You are not choosing dimensions — you are choosing among things that already have dimensions, and the skill is knowing which ones to walk away from.

So the honest first step is to write down what the front of the bar actually needs before you go looking: roughly forty-seven inches long, forty inches tall, wide six-inch cross boards, and nothing broken.

<figure><img src="/generated/pallet-backyard-bar/blog/chapter-1-2.png" alt="Four salvage pallets side by side with a spec card listing the requirements"><figcaption>Four candidates off the same pile, and a specification to measure them against instead of eyeballing it.</figcaption></figure>

Each requirement rejects something real. Forty inches of height is not decoration — it is the clearance a bar stool needs. Six-inch cross boards mean a face that reads as solid rather than as slats, and, just as importantly, wide targets to drive a screw into later. Split boards mean a pallet that already failed once under load.

<figure><img src="/generated/pallet-backyard-bar/blog/chapter-1-6.png" alt="Three candidates struck out, the fourth ringed in green as the bar front"><figcaption>One survivor: forty-seven inches of clean six-inch boards, in excellent condition.</figcaption></figure>

The payoff for that patience is the quiet claim in the original article — that by choosing pallets well, you can avoid cutting almost entirely. The front of this bar is never cut at all. It gets sanded lightly, "just enough so no one would get splinters," which is a deliberately low bar: the weathered, well-travelled look is the reason you are using pallets in the first place.

<figure><img src="/generated/pallet-backyard-bar/blog/chapter-1-9.png" alt="The chosen pallet standing on its long edge with 47-inch and 40-inch dimensions and a bar stool beside it"><figcaption>Stood on its long edge, the pallet is 47 inches of bar face at exactly stool height.</figcaption></figure>

## Chapter 2 · Two Cuts Make a U

Seen from above, the whole carcass is three pallets and one shape. The front is the one already chosen. The sides come from two more, picked to match its height exactly, and left as they are they would make a bar nearly four feet deep.

<figure><img src="/generated/pallet-backyard-bar/blog/chapter-2-2.png" alt="Plan view: the front pallet with two full-length side pallets running far back"><figcaption>Uncut side pallets would put the back of the bar four feet from the front of it.</figcaption></figure>

Two feet is the number the article settles on, and it is the only cutting the frame requires: one cut per side pallet, both to the same line. Then comes the detail that separates a build that looks finished from one that looks salvaged — each side is turned so its raw cut end faces forward, where the overhanging top will run out over it and hide it. The finished end stays visible at the back.

<figure><img src="/generated/pallet-backyard-bar/blog/chapter-2-5.png" alt="Both sides trimmed to two feet with their cut ends turned toward the front"><figcaption>Trimmed to 2 ft, cut ends forward, offcuts gone.</figcaption></figure>

Deck screws fasten the sides to the back of the front pallet — driven through the stringers, where the thick wood is, since a screw into one of a pallet's many gaps holds nothing. What you end up with is a U: forty-seven inches across the face, two feet deep on each wing, and open at the back so a person can stand inside it.

<figure><img src="/generated/pallet-backyard-bar/blog/chapter-2-9.png" alt="Side elevation showing a lone panel tipping over, then held upright by a two-foot wing"><figcaption>The wings are structural: on its own the front is a fence panel, braced it is furniture.</figcaption></figure>

## Chapter 3 · The Top That Ties It Together

The bar top for the front is not built. It is an existing wooden shelf, sixty-five inches by seventeen and a half — a suspiciously convenient size, until you add up the face it has to cover.

Forty-seven inches of front pallet, plus five more for each side pallet standing behind it, plus four inches of overhang past each end. Forty-seven plus ten plus eight is sixty-five. The dimension is not arbitrary; it is the sum of everything underneath it.

<figure><img src="/generated/pallet-backyard-bar/blog/chapter-3-3.png" alt="The 65-inch top with its width derived tick by tick: 4 + 5 + 37 + 5 + 4"><figcaption>Where 65 inches comes from — the carcass plus a 4-inch overhang at each end.</figcaption></figure>

Toward the customer it hangs over by eight inches, and that overhang is what makes it a bar rather than a shelf: room for elbows above and knees below when a stool pulls in. The side tops are the only lumber bought for the entire project — one board, cut in half, giving two pieces of seventeen and a half by nine and a half, one per wing.

Fastening is where a pallet build can quietly go wrong. The original instruction is to screw through the top "making sure to secure it to a board" — because a pallet is half air, and a screw that lands between boards grips nothing at all.

<figure><img src="/generated/pallet-backyard-bar/blog/chapter-3-6.png" alt="The top rendered semi-transparent so the stringers below are visible, with good and bad screw positions marked"><figcaption>Look through the top before you drill: three screws into solid stringers, one into a gap.</figcaption></figure>

And then the part that surprises people. Screws at two joints let the wings swing; the unfinished U leans like a cardboard box with no lid. The top spans all three pallets at once and fixes every joint at a set distance from every other, so the wobble has nowhere left to go. The article puts it plainly — attaching the top reinforces the structure. It is not a surface sitting on a frame. It is the frame's last member.

<figure><img src="/generated/pallet-backyard-bar/blog/chapter-3-8.png" alt="The top back on, joints locked, the racking gone"><figcaption>With the lid on, the shape is rigid.</figcaption></figure>

## Chapter 4 · Three Coats and a Level Patio

Everything left is about weather, and it is decided at the surface of the wood. Stain goes on with a brush, and the first coat mostly vanishes — dry, thirsty pallet wood drinks it in. The second and third have less bare wood left to soak into, so more stays near the surface and the colour finally evens out. Three coats is the number the article lands on.

<figure><img src="/generated/pallet-backyard-bar/blog/chapter-4-3.png" alt="A magnified slice through the wood, stain deepening across three coats"><figcaption>Coat by coat, with diminishing returns: each pass has less raw grain to disappear into.</figcaption></figure>

But stain is colour, not armour. It lives in the grain, and so does anything else that lands on the wood — which is why the bar top alone gets a coat of Varathane. That cures as a film sitting on top of the wood rather than inside it, and a film is what makes a surface washable.

<figure><img src="/generated/pallet-backyard-bar/blog/chapter-4-5.png" alt="A film of Varathane on the surface with a water drop beading on top of it"><figcaption>On stained wood a spill soaks in and leaves a ring; on the film it beads and wipes away.</figcaption></figure>

One problem is left, and it is underneath. Set on lawn, the bar sinks, rocks, and wicks moisture up into the wood you just spent three coats protecting — so the sod comes out, the ground is levelled, and patio stones go down where the bar will stand.

<figure><img src="/generated/pallet-backyard-bar/blog/chapter-4-8.png" alt="The finished bar standing on levelled patio stones with a stool at each end"><figcaption>Dry, flat and firm — the three things every outdoor build ends up asking for.</figcaption></figure>

Two bar stools slide under the overhang and a bottle opener gets screwed to the front, and that is the moment it stops being a project. The last honest note from the article is a maintenance one: a brushed outdoor finish is a subscription, not a purchase. Plan on recoating yearly, or spend once on an epoxy coating and stop thinking about it.

<figure><img src="/generated/pallet-backyard-bar/blog/chapter-4-10.png" alt="A one-year timeline showing the finish wearing and being recoated"><figcaption>A year later, the film is due again.</figcaption></figure>
