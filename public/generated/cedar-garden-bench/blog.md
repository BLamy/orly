# The Cedar Bench

*Weekend Builds №3 — grounded in Lee Valley's [How to Build a Contemporary Cedar Garden Bench](https://www.leevalley.com/en-ca/discover/woodworking/2020/july/how-to-build-contemporary-cedar-garden-bench) (July 2020): seven 2×4s, a cutting schedule, and a bench built by stacking.*

## Chapter 1 · Seven Boards, Four Lengths

The entire bench is seven two-by-fours, eight feet long, in cedar — with pressure-treated lumber or spruce as the cheaper substitutes, since nothing about the build depends on the species.

Before any real cut, an eighth of an inch comes off one end of each board. Lumberyard ends are rarely square, and every measurement in the project is taken from that end.

<figure><img src="/generated/cedar-garden-bench/blog/chapter-1-2.png" alt="Seven eight-foot boards, the first one trimmed for a clean edge"><figcaption>Seven boards, and an eighth of an inch off one end of each so the measuring can start from something square.</figcaption></figure>

Then the cutting schedule does its work. The first board yields a 48, an 18 and two 14½s — ninety-five inches of parts out of ninety-six inches of board. That is not a coincidence; the design was drawn around the lumber it is made of.

The next four boards are the same board again, and that repetition is exactly what a stop block is for. Clamped to the fence once, it makes every 14½-inch piece the same 14½ inches rather than a measurement taken five separate times.

<figure><img src="/generated/cedar-garden-bench/blog/chapter-1-4.png" alt="Five boards cut identically against a stop block, with the schedule labelled on each part"><figcaption>Set the stop block once, cut five: identical parts beat carefully measured ones.</figcaption></figure>

The sixth board gives up a 48 and two more 14½s; the seventh is the odd one out, supplying the small 3½-inch blocks along with the offcuts of everything before it. Count what is on the bench at the end: six pieces at 48 inches, five at 18, twelve at 14½, and five little blocks. Four lengths, twenty-eight parts, and a handful of short ends.

<figure><img src="/generated/cedar-garden-bench/blog/chapter-1-6.png" alt="The full tally: 6 × 48, 5 × 18, 12 × 14½, 5 × 3½"><figcaption>The whole bill of materials — four lengths and nothing else.</figcaption></figure>

## Chapter 2 · Clamp It Before You Glue It

The smallest meaningful piece of this bench is one 48-inch length with an 18-inch leg under each end. The article's advice is to build it with clamps and nothing else first — a full dry assembly, no glue, no screws.

<figure><img src="/generated/cedar-garden-bench/blog/chapter-2-2.png" alt="A rail and two legs held together with clamps only"><figcaption>Ten minutes of dry assembly buys the only look you get at the real thing before it is permanent.</figcaption></figure>

That is where you find the piece sitting an eighth proud, or the leg on backwards. In a clamped assembly it is a shrug; in a glued one it is a new board. It is also when the level goes on, and keeps going on — the article says to check level frequently, and a bench that is subtly out of level announces itself later, when a glass slides toward one end.

<figure><img src="/generated/cedar-garden-bench/blog/chapter-2-4.png" alt="A level laid across the dry-assembled rail with the bubble centring"><figcaption>Level checked while the joint is still adjustable.</figcaption></figure>

The other thing the dry run shows you is what is about to become unreachable. The inside of every leg and the underside of every rail vanish the moment the stack closes up, which is why the instruction is to sand every piece *before* final assembly. Sanding loose parts feels backwards; it is the difference between a bench that is smooth and one that is only smooth on top.

<figure><img src="/generated/cedar-garden-bench/blog/chapter-2-5.png" alt="Faces highlighted that will be inaccessible after assembly"><figcaption>Sand these now — no sander reaches them again.</figcaption></figure>

Only then does anything become permanent: glue, the same clamps in the same places, 2-inch pressure-treated screws through the first layer, and then the least popular instruction in woodworking — leave the clamps on for a full twenty-four hours.

<figure><img src="/generated/cedar-garden-bench/blog/chapter-2-8.png" alt="A 24-hour clock arc running while the clamps stay on"><figcaption>Glue that is dry to the touch in an hour is nowhere near strong yet.</figcaption></figure>

## Chapter 3 · The Stack Is the Joint

The first course is the whole bench in miniature: a 48-inch length, an 18-inch leg glued and clamped at each end, level checked, and a 3½-inch block screwed to the centre of the 48 with 2-inch pressure-treated screws. That tiny block is what keeps the long span from bowing inward.

From there the bench goes up in courses — a second 48 onto both legs, a 14½ onto the leg, another block, and so on. There is no mortise, no tenon, no dovetail anywhere in it. The layering *is* the joinery.

<figure><img src="/generated/cedar-garden-bench/blog/chapter-3-3.png" alt="The stack building upward, course by course, with glue lines between layers"><figcaption>Eight courses of 2×4, each glued, clamped and screwed to the one below.</figcaption></figure>

Partway up, the article switches from 2-inch screws to 2½- or 3-inch ones, and the geometry says exactly why. A screw has to cross everything it passes through and still bite about an inch into the piece it lands in. Through one layer of inch-and-a-half stock, two inches is barely enough; through two layers it is not enough at all.

<figure><img src="/generated/cedar-garden-bench/blog/chapter-3-5.png" alt="A gauge comparing required screw length against the stock it must pass through"><figcaption>Two layers of 1½-inch stock plus an inch of bite is four inches of reach — a 2-inch screw never gets there.</figcaption></figure>

The rest is repetition: interior pieces stacked, then the final outside 48 glued and clamped over the whole face and a last 14½ on the outer leg. A stack like this is far stronger than it looks, because a glue joint on a long-grain face is stronger than the wood on either side of it — and every course adds another entire face of it.

<figure><img src="/generated/cedar-garden-bench/blog/chapter-3-8.png" alt="Every glue face in the stack highlighted at once"><figcaption>The strength is not in the fasteners; it is in the accumulated glue area.</figcaption></figure>

## Chapter 4 · Dowels, Not Screw Heads

Which leaves the screw holes. This design does not hide them — it plugs each one with a dowel and then makes the arrangement of plugs into the pattern that defines the bench.

Every mark sits one inch in from the edge, far enough that the drill will not split out the side. The hole is ⅜ of an inch wide and half an inch deep — not through, just deep enough to sink the head and leave a clean round socket. The screw is driven from the bottom of that socket, which means it starts half an inch further in than it appears, one more reason the fasteners had to get longer.

<figure><img src="/generated/cedar-garden-bench/blog/chapter-4-3.png" alt="Cross-section of the countersink with the screw driven from its base"><figcaption>⅜ inch wide, ½ inch deep, and the screw begins at the bottom of it.</figcaption></figure>

A ⅜-inch dowel is glued into the socket, left standing proud, and trimmed once the glue sets with a Japanese saw — a saw that cuts on the pull stroke and whose blade flexes flat against the surface, so it takes the dowel off without gouging the wood around it. Then two sanders in order: the belt sander to level the plug and the surface into one plane, and the orbital afterwards to remove everything the belt just did.

<figure><img src="/generated/cedar-garden-bench/blog/chapter-4-6.png" alt="The pull saw trimming the proud dowel flush with the face"><figcaption>Flush-cut on the pull stroke, then belt, then orbital.</figcaption></figure>

Pull back and the detail becomes the design. On the legs, the holes sit three inches down from the top and three up from the bottom. Along the top length, the marks go 1¾ inches in from the edge — and that number is not a style choice. A 2×4 is 3½ inches wide, so 1¾ is exactly half of it: the dowels land dead centre over the wood beneath them.

<figure><img src="/generated/cedar-garden-bench/blog/chapter-4-8.png" alt="The outer face with dowel positions marked on the legs"><figcaption>Three inches from each end of the leg — spacing you can see from across a garden.</figcaption></figure>

<figure><img src="/generated/cedar-garden-bench/blog/chapter-4-10.png" alt="The finished face with an even row of dowel plugs along the top"><figcaption>1¾ inches is half of 3½ — the dowels sit on the centreline of the stock below.</figcaption></figure>

Seven boards, four lengths, eight glued courses and a grid of dowels. The fasteners stopped being something to apologise for, which is the whole reason this bench reads as contemporary rather than rustic.
