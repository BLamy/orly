# WHO TAUGHT THE MACHINE?
### A case history of neural networks, 1943–2026

**Final treatment (head writer).** The winning "mystery" treatment, rebuilt with the judges' grafts from "What We Were Sure Of" (beliefs) and "The Long Relay" (people).

**Sources:** `research/bible.md`, `claims.json`, `quotes.json` only. Claim ids are in square brackets. Quote ids start with `q`. Every quotation in the film is copied verbatim from `quotes.json`; everything in a narrator's sentence that is not a quote is a paraphrase of a claim.

## 0. What changed from the winning treatment

The mystery's spine stays: a machine writes back, nobody wrote it a rulebook, so who taught it? An unseen old teacher works the case forward from a lamp in Chicago, and in the last minute he steps back and sees that his case board has been shaped like a neural network all along. The judges asked for fewer devices, clearer held images and better ways to show *how long* beliefs lasted. So:

1. **Four recurring devices only.** The Case Board, the Belief Candles (now a true candle-clock, staged with the *Edge of the Light*), the X-ray chat box, and one spoken refrain, "Put that in the file." The numbered Exhibits are gone; every machine gets an era-adaptive **date stamp** and nothing more. The courtroom Witness Box is gone; its testimony moves to the rim of each candle's light.
2. **The Case Board absorbs two small layers.** Open-question cards hang on **red thread** across the top of the board and come down when answered (from "people"). The **Wall of Forecasts** is pinned beside the board, and prediction slips age visibly on it (from "beliefs").
3. **The candle-clock makes "how long" countable.** One scored ring per year, one fixed scale for the whole film. In the last minute the camera pulls back and the shelf is revealed as **a bar chart in wax**, with the honest caveat that the newest candles are short partly because they haven't finished burning. DeepSeek's day is a **struck match**, not a candle.
4. **The deep dives use the five-beat grammar.** Belief → machine → worked number → the night it was tested → what lasted. Each runs about five minutes but holds only 400–420 words, so the machines play in silence with music.
5. **Accuracy fixes first.** Hinton is never staged standing in the 1970s or 1980s. He enters at the edge of the light at a desk in Sussex, 1976 [e4-hinton-cv]; he is shown standing only from the mid-2000s, and the posture is first made a point of at Lake Tahoe in December 2012 [e8-hinton-back]. Anthropic's founders are described with no number and no implied count [e11-anthropic-founding]. The subtitle no longer says "a mystery in reverse", because the telling is chronological.
6. **Chapters 13–14 of the mystery became three chapters** (The Case Reopened · Two Shores · Case Status: Open) built from a few held images each, not lists. The Nobel moves into the final chapter's fast-forward.

## 1. Logline

A machine answers you in fluent sentences, and nobody ever wrote it a rulebook. So who taught it? An old teacher takes the case. Starting from a lamp in Chicago in 1943, he follows it forward clue by clue. In every era clever people were sure of something; he lights a candle for each certainty and lets it burn for exactly as many years as it lasted. The doubters stand at the edge of its light, and over eighty years we watch people walk from the edge to the centre and back again. In the end he finds that the culprit was never one person. When he steps back from his case board, he sees it has been shaped like a neural network all along. One lantern in it is still dark.

**Title:** *Who Taught the Machine?* **Subtitle:** *A case history of neural networks, 1943–2026.*

## 3. Recurring devices

There are four, and they are always used the same way, so that by chapter 3 a fifteen-year-old can read an era at a glance: **the board** says what was learned, **the candle** says what was believed and for how long, **the X-ray** says which part of today's machine came from it, and **the refrain** marks a clue that will matter later.

### 3.1 The Case Board (the spine and the final twist)

A dark corkboard in the detective's study, lit by a green-shaded desk lamp (candleAmber). The detective is **only two weathered hands and a lamp**. We never see his body, so he can never be mistaken for any real person in the film, Hinton least of all [bible §9.8]. The hands work with index cards, pins, red thread, a magnifying glass and a fountain pen.

- **Clue cards.** Each chapter ends with the hands pinning one paper-cut object card: a lantern with a wick (1943), a knob (1958), a pair of scissors making two cuts (1969), a bead of light running backwards on a string (1986), a pierced tin stencil (1989), two small furnaces (2012), a thread of light between two word-slips (2017), a single type sort (2018), a pair of garden shears (2022). Threads of light tie each new card to the cards that made it possible. Backprop ties to the knob and to a small Finnish thesis; AlexNet ties to the stencil, the furnaces and the ImageNet card; the Transformer card ties back to a Toronto card through Aidan Gomez [e10-gomez].
- **Date stamps instead of exhibits.** Every machine enters with an era-adaptive stamp, bottom left: Special Elite typewriter (1940s–60s), VT323 phosphor (1970s–80s), IBM Plex Mono (1990s on). For example *BUFFALO · 1958*. There are no exhibit numbers.
- **The red thread of questions.** Open-question cards hang on red thread across the top of the board. Each comes down, and drops into the lamp's pool, when it is answered. They give a teacher natural pause points.

**The final twist (ch 15).** The camera pulls back. The clue cards, from the 1943 wick to the 2022 shears, sit in columns joined by every thread pinned over thirty minutes. The threads tighten, the cards turn into paper lanterns, and **it is a lantern network**. A real forward wave of light (`lanternNetwork`) runs from the 1943 wick to the chat box at the output. The answer to "who taught it?" is the shape of the board. One lantern in the middle stays dark, and the last question card hangs over it.

### 3.2 The Belief Candles: a candle-clock, staged at the Edge of the Light

**The shelf.** Below the board runs a long paper-cut shelf. Each belief of the age is one tall candle, **scored with one ring per year at a single fixed scale for the whole film** (14 px per ring at L3). It is lit when the belief takes hold; the flame travels down one ring for each year the belief holds; when the evidence ends it, the flame gutters, a thread of smoke rises and the stub stays on the shelf with a small tag. Where the bible calls an end date interpretive, the stub has a soft, frayed rim and its tag carries "c." The belief is lettered on the candle in its era's typeface as a paraphrase in small caps, never in quotation marks, so it can never be mistaken for a quote card.

**The stage.** When a candle is lit, the camera pushes into its flame and through a lamp-change into the era itself, where the same candle stands huge on a paper stage and throws a pool of light across the floor. **The candle and the people share one pool of light.** The holders of the belief stand inside it, facing the flame. **The dissenters stand at the rim, half in shadow, facing out, their long shadows across the floor.** The rule never changes: *whoever doubts stands at the edge.* Testimony from the old Witness Box now happens here, as quote cards that rise beside a figure at the rim. Over eighty years we see people move, and that movement shows how knowledge travels without a word of narration:

- **Minsky** stands in the centre of SNARC's light in 1951 (ch 2) and at the rim of 1958's (ch 3) [e1-snarc, e1-minsky-switch, e2-minsky-1961].
- **Hinton** first appears at the rim in Sussex, 1976, seated at a desk (ch 4) [e4-hinton-cv]. He stays at the rim through desks in San Diego, Cambridge, Pittsburgh and Toronto (ch 5–7). He is shown standing only from the mid-2000s, and in December 2012 he stands at the centre of the new candle's light (ch 9) [e8-hinton-back]. In May 2023 he walks out to the rim again, doubting the safety of his own field's success (ch 13).
- **LeCun** is at the rim through the quiet years (ch 6), at the centre after Florence, and at the rim again by 2023–24 as the leading doubter of doom and of language models as the road (ch 13, 15) [e12-lecun-2023, e14-lecun].
- **Bengio** stands at the rim in the worst years (ch 6) and in 2023 stands inside the new candle of worry (ch 13) [e12-cais].
- In every winter the dissenters at the rim carry small hand-lanterns, and those lanterns light the next candle.

**The bar chart in wax (ch 15).** The pull-back that turns the board into a network keeps going down to the shelf: board above, shelf below, in one camera move. For the first time the shelf is seen whole. Tall stubs from the early decades, shorter ones later, one blackened match, and four flames still burning. The narrator adds the honest caveat: *the newest candles are short partly because they haven't finished burning.*

**The leitmotif (handed to `/film-score`).** Every candle has the same three-note figure, re-orchestrated for its era (celesta in the 1940s, valve organ for the 1950s labs, solo cello in the winters, a pulsing synth for the GPU years, a neutral plucked string split evenly across the stereo field for Two Shores, with no national-instrument clichés):
1. a **rising fourth** = lit and held;
2. a **held suspension** = doubted;
3. a **falling resolution** = gone.

A belief still burning stops on the suspension and does not resolve. **The film's last chord is that suspension.** Cue boundaries land on candle lightings, so "how long" becomes something you hear.

### 3.3 The Suspect's Anatomy: the chat box, X-rayed

The glowing rectangle from the first minute comes back near the end of most chapters for 4–6 seconds, without narration. Each time it is a little more translucent, and one more of its real components lights inside it: threshold units (ch 2) → adjustable weights (ch 3) → hidden layers and a backward arrow (ch 5) → racks of graphics cards and a sea of text (ch 7) → attention threads (ch 10) → next-word type sorts (ch 11) → a pair of preference shears (ch 12). **Only things really present in a modern chat model light up.** Convolution, the Mark I's random wiring and the SVM never do, and the narrator says so once, in ch 8: *"Not every clue is a part of the suspect. Some are the tools that trained the detectives."* At the return to the scene (ch 12) the box is fully lit, and the prologue's question has a physical answer.

### 3.4 The refrain: "Put that in the file."

Spoken five times, never more, each time over a small fact that will matter later, as the hands drop a card into a manila file:
1. ch 1 — the launch post's own admission that it "sometimes writes plausible-sounding but incorrect or nonsensical answers" [q12-chatgpt-limits] (paid in ch 12);
2. ch 2 — the nets "could not learn" [q-mp-assumption5] (paid in ch 3);
3. ch 3 — Rosenblatt's 1961 sentence about language [q-rosenblatt-linguistic] (paid in ch 12);
4. ch 5 — Cyc's 1985 "practically nothing… from the raw text" [q-lenat-rawtext] (paid in ch 11);
5. ch 8 — "simply by waiting for faster GPUs and bigger datasets" [q-alexnet-waiting] (paid in ch 11).

No other refrain is fixed. The narrator may vary "It burned for about ___ years" when a candle goes out, but it is a habit, not a slogan.

### 3.5 Bookends (not recurring)

- **The Rewind (ch 1).** Five images run backwards in ten seconds, unexplained: a Go stone lifts off a board, snow rises into the sky, a motorised knob unturns, two fans in a bedroom tower spin down, a lantern wick goes dark. It lands on *CHICAGO · 1943*.
- **The Fast-Forward (ch 15).** The same five images run forwards, now understood: the knob (1958), the snow (1969), the fans (2012), the Go stone (2016), the wick catching inside the chat box. One line: "Every one of those was a clue."

## 4. Voice of the narrator

An unseen, unnamed elderly British academic. He is an archetype, not a real person, and he must never sound like or claim to be Geoffrey Hinton, who appears in the film as a character [bible §9.8]. He is slow and warm, wry about certainty (his own included), precise about numbers, fond of everyone in the story, and never cruel about a mistake. He speaks to a curious fifteen-year-old and an expert at the same time.

**Rules for the ear.**
- Short sentences. One idea per sentence. A pause is a tool; mark it with a line break or `pauseAfter`.
- "We" and "you", never "I was there". He may say "I" only as a guide ("Hold on to that").
- Numbers spoken as people say them ("about fifteen in a hundred", "sixty million"). Exact figures, symbols and equations go **on screen**, never into the ear.
- No acronyms in a row. Say "a graphics card", not "GPU", the first time; say "Alex Net" (lexicon), never spell it.
- Quotes are introduced with "he wrote", "she said", "the paper says", and then read **verbatim** from `quotes.json`. A paraphrase never sounds like a quote.
- Every chapter ends on a **turn**, not a summary: a small reversal, a question, or an image that opens the next chapter.
- Wry, never sarcastic. The joke is always on certainty, never on a person.
- No villains. Every belief gets its evidence before its dissenter, and its dissenter before its ending [bible §9.3–9.4].

**Sample lines that set the tone** (each traces to the claims shown):

1. "Here is a small wonder. You type a question into a little lit box, and something that is not a person writes back. In whole sentences. One word at a time." *(ch 1)*
2. "At least seven hundred million people use machines like this every week. And nobody ever wrote it a rulebook for English." *(ch 1; e14-bengio)*
3. "These nets could think, of a kind. But they could not learn. Put that in the file." *(ch 2; q-mp-assumption5, e1-mp-nolearn)*
4. "It was a lovely theorem, with a small word hidden inside it. The word was 'if'." *(ch 3; e2-convergence)*
5. "Nobody in the warm houses was looking at these windows. That is what a winter is." *(ch 4)*
6. "The most important clue in this case was sitting in a thesis about something else." *(ch 4; e3-linnainmaa)*
7. "One clean bowl. No fog. No traps. A proof that you'll reach the bottom. So why would anyone choose the fog?" *(ch 6; e6-svm)*
8. "All three conditions, they wrote, were now satisfied. They were nearly right." *(ch 7; q-hs-threeconditions)*
9. "Nobody told the two towers to divide the work. They did it anyway. Every time." *(ch 8; e8-gpu-split)*
10. "In 1985, that was a perfectly sensible thing to believe." *(ch 11; q-lenat-rawtext)*
11. "Persistence is not the same as certainty. In this whole story, he is the only one we see take down his own forecast." *(ch 13; q12-hinton-wayoff)*
12. "The case is not closed. It never is. Somewhere tonight a lamp is on, and someone is suspicious. Perhaps it's you." *(ch 15)*

**Lexicon starters** (for `voice/lexicon.json`): McCulloch "muh-CULL-uck"; Papert "pa-PAIR"; Linnainmaa "LIN-nigh-mah"; Rumelhart "ROO-mel-hart"; LeCun "luh-KUN"; Hochreiter "HOKE-rye-ter"; Cireșan "chee-reh-SHAHN"; Krizhevsky "kri-ZHEF-skee" (said once at most); Sutskever "soots-KEV-er"; Uszkoreit "OOSH-koh-rite"; Bahdanau "bah-DAH-now"; Polosukhin "po-lo-SOO-khin"; Shazeer "sha-ZEER"; Liang Wenfeng "lyahng wun-fung"; AlexNet "Alex Net"; ImageNet "Image Net"; "GPU" as "graphics card" or "G-P-U" after first use.

### 1 · The Wonder in the Dark Room (1:30 · 150 words) · 2026 → 1943

### 2 · Clue One: A Switch That Fires (1:45 · 175 words) · 1941–1959 · candleAmber
- **Chicago, about 1941.** A teenager of about eighteen is introduced to a doctor of forty-two, who takes him into his family home [e1-pitts-age]. (The *Principia* library story may be used only as "by one biographer's account" [e1-pitts-principia].)
- **1943.** Their neuron is a switch: all or none [e1-mp-paper, q-mp-allornone]. A single paper lantern whose wick catches only when the light reaching it rises above a cut-line ring. Three lanterns make AND, then OR.
- **The missing piece.** The fifth assumption on a card: "The structure of the net does not change with time." [q-mp-assumption5, e1-mp-nolearn] The first red-thread card goes up: *How would a machine change its own wiring?* "Put that in the file."
- **The candle is lit:** THE BRAIN IS A LOGIC MACHINE. McCulloch and Pitts stand in its pool. At the rim, the neurophysiologist Ralph Gerard argues that the brain works more like a dial than a switch (paraphrase) [e1-gerard]. Most brain scientists barely noticed [e1-mp-ripple].
- **1949–1950.** Hebb's postulate on a quote card, a thread thickening between two lanterns each time one helps fire the other [e1-hebb, q-hebb-postulate]; the narrator notes that "fire together, wire together" came later and is not his wording [e1-hebb-notslogan]. Turing's "child machine", to be educated, goes in the file [q-turing-child, e1-turing-1950], and his "about fifty years" goes up as the first forecast slip [q-turing-fifty].
- **1951.** SNARC: about forty "neurons", three hundred tubes, a surplus bomber autopilot turning the knobs [e1-snarc]. Young Minsky stands **in the centre** of the light. "We sort of quit science for a while to watch the machine." [q-minsky-snarc-watch]

## 6. How the deep dives teach

Both deep dives use the **five-beat grammar**: (1) the belief, and why it was reasonable → (2) the machine, built one part at a time → (3) a worked number on a parchment plate → (4) the night it was tested → (5) what lasted. Each opens with a one-line **teaching promise**. Every visual variable is bound to a real quantity (style bible §6). Each step is one idea and one machine motion, then a held beat; exact symbols go on screen, and the narration says them in words. Word counts are deliberately low so the machines can play in silence under music.

## 12. Fairness and accuracy guardrails for the chapter writers

- **Only the bible.** Every fact carries a claim id in `shot.claims`. Quotes only from `quotes.json`, verbatim, on quote cards with speaker and date; candle letterings and belief plates are paraphrases in small caps with no quotation marks. Fragments must be exact substrings.
- **Contested items get two voices or the bible's safe wording:** the bedroom (Computer History Museum vs NVIDIA), the $44 million auction (Cade Metz), Pitts and the *Principia* (one biographer), *Perceptrons* "killing" nets, backprop priority, IDSIA vs Toronto, the Lighthill debate's date, "too dangerous to release" (a headline, not the company's words), Gebru's exit, Lemoine, Altman's removal, emergence, the risk camps, DeepSeek's cost, the gap, distillation and smuggling, Glasswing's claims, the Anthropic litigation (both courts).
- **Hinton staging:** never standing in the 1970s or 1980s; seated at desks at the rim from Sussex 1976; standing only from the mid-2000s, and made a point of from Lake Tahoe, December 2012 [e8-hinton-back, e4-hinton-cv]. Not present in Florence [e8-hinton-absent]. The narrator never sounds like him.
- **Krizhevsky** is never given words or views, and no birthplace [bible §9].
- **Anthropic** gets exactly the standard every lab gets: founding facts with no founder count, self-description as self-description, FTX money, Constitutional AI with its caveat, Glasswing as company claims, both courts, the copyright settlement, the distillation allegations as allegations, and the end-card disclosure. No heroic framing anywhere.
